India and Southeast Asia

Sanson, Nicolas, 1600-1667; Sanson, Guillaume (1633-1703) · 1703

Sanson’s map of southern India ‘in two peninsulas’, one on either side of the Ganges. A disciplined French engraving retains the ancient conceptual division between India within and beyond the Ganges.

The full title as argument

Partie Méridionale de l’Inde en deux presqu’îles, l’une deçà et l’autre delà le Gange names its organising scheme explicitly. The Indian peninsula and mainland Southeast Asia are treated as the two peninsulas of a larger India.

An ancient division in modern form

The distinction descends from Ptolemaic geography, yet the sheet is engraved in the restrained style of the French school and populated with early-modern place knowledge. It therefore demonstrates how older conceptual categories survived within increasingly critical cartography.

Coasts and interiors

Maritime edges, ports and major political names receive the clearest treatment. Inland information is thinner and more uneven. The map’s apparent clarity should not be mistaken for uniform empirical knowledge across the territory it represents.

The gaze

Europe has updated what it knows without fully changing how it divides the region. India is modernised inside an ancient frame—a reminder that cartographic progress consists as much in persistent categories as in corrected coastlines.
