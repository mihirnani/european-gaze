About the Collection

About

A study collection of 66 foreign maps of India, 1519–1946, with a 1947 epilogue — and an argument about the eye behind them.

For more than four centuries, maps made for European courts, publishers, trading companies, survey departments, colonial states and mass publics represented India — first as a coastline and a rumour, then as the empire of the Great Mogul, then as a territory to be surveyed, administered and, at the end of British rule, partitioned. This collection gathers 66 of those maps and reads them less for what they record about the land than for what they reveal about the institutions and audiences for which they were made: what they wanted, what they could see, and what their methods left unseen.

The scope

This is not a history of cartography in India, and it does not suggest that India entered cartographic knowledge when Europeans began to draw it. It is a study of one foreign archive. “The European gaze” is used as a curatorial shorthand, not as though Europe possessed a single eye: Portuguese pilots, French geographers, Dutch and German publishers, East India Company surveyors, imperial departments and twentieth-century newspaper artists looked for different things and worked under different constraints.

The argument

Set in sequence, the maps trace a broad shift from inheritance and report toward measurement and administration. India is fitted to Ptolemy, approached from the sea, organised around Mughal sovereignty and commercial routes, triangulated and surveyed, and eventually broken into districts, censuses, thematic layers and proposed boundaries. The sequence is a curatorial argument rather than a claim of simple linear progress: decorative, commercial, scholarly, navigational and administrative forms overlapped throughout the period.

Survey and co-production

The authority of the colonial survey often appears on the finished sheet as the achievement of a named European surveyor. The measurements, place-names and routes on which it depended were also produced through extensive Indian labour and local knowledge — by chainmen, signal builders, draftsmen, guides, interpreters, informants and subordinate survey establishments whose names rarely entered the atlas record. The collection treats the map as an imperial institutional object without mistaking its production for a solitary European act.

How it is organised

The maps are arranged in seven sections: Antiquity and Renaissance; Baroque, Mughals and Companies; The Survey Turn; Home Ground: Bombay and the Deccan; Administered Empire and Victorian Atlas; the maritime interlude The Sea and the Route; and Last Frontiers. The order is broadly chronological but grouped by theme, so each section makes a smaller argument within the larger one. The Bombay and Deccan room is deliberately situated: it gives extra attention to the collector’s own home ground rather than pretending to be a geographically neutral survey of India.

The 1947 epilogue

The formal collection ends in 1946, with British partition planning and the Portuguese state’s demographic mapping of Goa, Daman and Diu. A September 1947 panorama of Punjab follows as an epilogue: not another plan for the imperial line, but a metropolitan image of the catastrophe after partition and independence.

On the descriptions

Each completed entry begins with the object itself — title, maker, date, form and visible evidence — before placing it within the collection’s argument. Provenance links and catalogue identifiers are supplied on the revised object pages where they have been verified. Uncertain dates, attributions and institutional references are marked as such rather than silently converted into fact.

Sources and terms

Images remain credited to their holding collections and source records. The collection is open and non-commercial, assembled for study and teaching. It claims no rights in the historical maps or in third-party digital images, whose reuse remains subject to the terms stated by the source institution.
