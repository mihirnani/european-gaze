About the Collection

About

Foreign maps of India, 1519–1946, with a 1947 epilogue – and what they reveal about the eye behind them.

For four centuries Europeans drew India: first as a coastline and a rumour, then as the empire of the Great Mogul, then as a territory to be surveyed, administered and, at the end, partitioned. This collection gathers a selection of those maps and reads them less for what they record about the land than for what they reveal about the people who made them – what they wanted, what they could see, and what their methods left unseen.

What it is not

It is not a history of cartography in India, and it does not suggest that India was unmapped until Europeans arrived. It is a study of one foreign archive. “The European gaze” is shorthand: Portuguese pilots, French geographers, Dutch publishers, Company surveyors and newspaper artists looked for different things.

How it is arranged

Seven rooms, broadly chronological but grouped by theme: Antiquity and Renaissance; The Great Mogul and the Companies; The Survey Turn; Home Ground: Bombay and the Deccan; Administered Empire and Victorian Atlas; The Sea and the Route; and Last Frontiers. The Bombay room gives extra attention to the collector’s own home ground. A 1947 panorama of Punjab closes the sequence as an epilogue – not another plan for the imperial line, but a view of what followed it.

On the surveys

The finished sheet usually credits a named European surveyor. The measurements and place-names it rests on were also the work of Indian chainmen, signal-builders, draftsmen, guides and informants whose names rarely reached the atlas. The collection treats the map as an imperial object without pretending it was a solitary European act.

How the pages were written

The choice of which maps to hang is the collector’s. The commentary was first drafted with AI and is being rewritten by hand as time allows – the pages that read the plate closely are the rewritten ones; it has been cross-checked against the catalogue records and the standard histories, but no collection of this size can be guaranteed free of error, and corrections are welcome at the address below.

Maps published after 1929 may carry rights of their own, separate from the scan; the few here from that period are shown for study and criticism, and a rights-holder who objects may write to the address below. Uncertain dates and attributions are marked as such. The collection is open and non-commercial, assembled for study; it claims no rights in the maps themselves.
