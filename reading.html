A Reader’s Guide

How to read one of these

A short guide to the maps: what to look for on a sheet, shown on plates from the collection, and a note on the viewer.

Start at the cartouche

The cartouche is the framed panel that carries the title. It says who made the sheet, for whom, and in what spirit. On Rennell’s Hindoostan (1782) it is a political statement in miniature: Britannia before a pedestal of victories, kneeling pundits presenting a book labelled ‘Shaster’, the Hindu law, and sepoys looking on. Rennell glossed the scene as the ‘humane interposition of the British legislature in favor of the natives of Bengal’. Two centuries earlier Ortelius’s Indiae Orientalis (1570) used the space for strapwork and filled the sea with ships and monsters. The ornament is not idle: it tells the buyer what kind of object this is, and it occupies the paper the maker could not fill with knowledge. Read the cartouche as a claim, then test it against the sheet.

The scale bar and its units

Near the cartouche is a bar divided into units, and the units say who the map was for. Antwerp, Amsterdam and Nuremberg sheets give German miles, fifteen to a degree of latitude, about 7.4 km each. Portuguese and Spanish charts use leagues of seventeen and a half or eighteen to the degree, a little over 6 km. French sheets give the lieue marine of twenty to the degree (5.56 km) or the shorter common league of about 4.4 km. British maps of the survey era print ‘British statute miles, 69½ to a degree’, sometimes with sea leagues beside them. Indian distances reached the compilers in the coss or kos, a road measure that Rennell’s Memoir had to reckon with and that changed with the region: about 1.8 km in Bengal, over 4 km on the Mughal roads of the north where the kos-minars still stand, and a Mysore sultani kos of nearly 6 km. A kos on one sheet is not a kos on another. Murray’s Hampi plan (1911) has a bar of 880 yards, half a mile, the closest the collection comes to the ground. The scale fractions given on the pages here – the 1:4,600,000 of Rennell’s Hindoostan, for instance – are taken from the holding collection’s catalogue or computed from the sheet’s own bar and dimensions; the makers themselves stated none. Vandermaelen is the exception: his Atlas Universel was drawn throughout at 1:1,641,836 and says so.

Which zero

Longitude needs a zero, and there was no agreement on one until the Washington conference of 1884. Dutch, German and many French sheets count from Ferro, the westernmost of the Canaries, fixed by Louis XIII’s council in 1634 as the meridian for French maps and later defined as exactly 20° west of Paris – 17°40′ west of Greenwich. Van der Aa’s Golconda sheet (1706) runs from 85° to 125° east of Ferro, which puts Masulipatnam near 99°. French maps of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries count from the Paris Observatory, 2°20′ east of Greenwich; Vandermaelen’s Deccan tiles (1827) do, and the page says so: the Gujarat tile begins near 66° east of Paris, which is about 68°20′ east of Greenwich. British sheets count from London, later Greenwich; France kept Paris for its charts until 1914. A longitude read off a plate must be converted before it is compared: subtract about 17°40′ for Ferro, about 2°20′ for Paris.

Latitude good, longitude bad

Before about 1800 the coastline is nearly right and the interior is not. Latitude was easy: the height of the sun at noon, or of the pole star, gives it directly, and the Portuguese pilots behind the Miller Atlas leaf (1519) had it for every headland. Longitude needs time. At sea, before the chronometer, east–west position was dead reckoning, course and speed carried forward from the last fix, and it drifted. Harrison’s watch proved the chronometer in the 1760s and the Nautical Almanac’s lunar tables began in 1767, but chronometers stayed scarce until the 1820s. Inland the problem was worse. Distances came from itineraries in days and coss, weighed against a few astronomical fixes: d’Anville (1752) left the poorly known interior blank; Rennell filled Bengal from route surveys run with a perambulator and compass and joined the rest from report. The remedy was triangulation. From a baseline of seven and a half miles measured at St Thomas Mount, Madras, from 10 April 1802 with a hundred-foot chain, Lambton carried a chain of triangles across the peninsula to Mangalore, some 360 miles, and every position inside the net became geometry rather than testimony. The 1922 index shows the finished lattice.

Names

Spellings are the map’s accent. ‘Visiapour’ on the 1706 sheet and ‘Bejapoor’ on Vandermaelen’s tile are both Bijapur, heard by a Portuguese ear and an English one. ‘Golconde’, ‘Golcondæ’ and ‘Golkonda’ are one fort. ‘Bagnagar’, on seventeenth-century sheets, is Hyderabad under the name Tavernier also used for it. ‘Seringapatam’ is Srirangapatna. ‘Bisnagar’ and ‘Narsinga’ are Vijayanagara, still drawn as a living kingdom in 1706, a century and a half after Talikota. To look a name up, say it aloud, keep the consonants, and try them against the modern form; the Places index lists the variants that occur in this collection’s notes.

Rivers as borders

Early makers had rivers before they had roads, and a river is a convenient edge. On the Van der Aa sheet the Mughal empire stops at the Tapti; on others the Krishna and the Tungabhadra divide the sultanates from the south, as they did across the Raichur doab. On the administrative sheets that follow, the SDUK Bombay sheet (1856) or Bartholomew’s Bombay and Berar (1893), look for where a coloured boundary turns to follow a river. Treat a river-border with care: sometimes it records a frontier, sometimes only that the river was the one line the compiler could draw.

Colour

Until the middle of the nineteenth century colour was added by hand after printing, and outline colour, a wash along the borders only, was the cheap standard: Hall’s Hindoostan (1827) is coloured that way, and Wilkinson’s southern province (1800) is hand-coloured too; the wash marks possession, not terrain. Relief on such sheets is hachure, short engraved strokes down the slope. Colour printing changed what colour could say. On Mitchell’s Hindoostan (1857) the tints sort territory by its legal relation to Britain; on Bartholomew’s 1893 plates colour becomes quantity, graded bands for population density and layer tints for elevation on Bombay and Environs. On the Hampi plan the walls are red and the river green because the walker needs to find them. Ask of any wash what it encodes.

Dates

A plate carries a date; the knowledge on it is older. Van der Aa’s 1706 sheet draws the Golconda of Methold’s 1620s. The Linschoten impression shown is a 1638 printing of a 1596 chart. Faden’s peninsula is dated 1792 in the title and 1 May 1793 in the imprint. Mitchell’s Hindoostan was entered for copyright in 1856 and cannot be a comment on 1857. Wilkinson’s map of 1800 is here in an 1808 issue. Read three dates on any sheet: when the information was gathered, when the plate was cut, when this impression was printed. The first and the last are often a generation apart.

The viewer

Most plates open in a zoomable viewer built on tiled images. Scroll or pinch to zoom, drag to pan, double-click to zoom in a step; the viewer’s own buttons zoom, reset and go full-screen. Where tiles are not available the page shows the plain image inside a scrolling frame. The left and right arrow keys move to the previous and next plate in a room. Zoom to the cartouche first, then the scale bar, then the margins, where the graticule numbers say which zero the maker counted from.

Sources

The Ferro meridian: the 1634 decision and the later definition as 20° west of Paris (17°39′46″ west of Greenwich, rounded to 17°40′) follow the standard references on the Ferro and Paris meridians, including the 1884 conference and France’s adoption of Greenwich for navigation in 1914. Units: the German geographical mile at 1/15° (7.41 km); Portuguese and French leagues per the usual tables (18 to a degree, 6.17 km; lieue marine 5.556 km; lieue commune 4.45 km); ‘British statute miles, 69½ to a degree’ and ‘sea leagues, 20 to a degree’ as printed on Jefferys and Sayer sheets of the 1770s; the coss from Hobson-Jobson and later compilations (Bengal coss 6,000 feet; Akbar’s kos about 4.19 km from the British standardisation of the guz; kos-minar spacing about 4.17 km; Mysore sultani coss about 5.9 km). Rennell’s cartouche and his gloss on it: the Memoir of a Map of Hindoostan and Sabarish Suresh’s discussion in the Law and History Review (2024). Lambton: the Madras baseline of 10 April to 22 May 1802, 7.5 miles, 100-foot chain, and the 360-mile connection to Mangalore, from the standard accounts of the Great Trigonometrical Survey. Chronometers: Harrison’s H4 of 1761; Arnold and Earnshaw’s detent escapements about 1780; routine Royal Navy issue by 1825. Everything said about individual plates is taken from their pages in this collection.
